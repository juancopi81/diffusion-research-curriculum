## Strategic Practice 9 — Conditional Expectation Problem 1

**Prompt.**
You get to choose between two envelopes, each of which contains a check for some positive amount of money. Unlike in the two envelope paradox from class, it is not given that one envelope contains twice as much money as the other envelope. Instead, assume that the two values were generated independently from some distribution on positive real numbers, with no information given about what that distribution is.

After picking an envelope, you can open it and see how much money is inside (call this value $x$), and then you have the option of switching. As no information has been given about the distribution, it may seem impossible to have better than a $50\%$ chance of picking the better envelope. Intuitively, we may want to switch if $x$ is "small" and not switch if $x$ is "large," but how do we define "small" and "large" in the grand scheme of all possible distributions?

Consider the following strategy for deciding whether to switch. Generate a "threshold" $T \sim \mathrm{Expo}(1)$, and switch envelopes if and only if the observed value $x$ is less than the value of $T$. Show that this strategy succeeds in picking the envelope with more money with probability strictly greater than $1/2$.

Hint: Let $t$ be the value of $T$ generated by a random draw from the $\mathrm{Expo}(1)$ distribution. First explain why the strategy works very well if $t$ happens to be in between the two envelope values, and does no harm in any case; that is, there is no case in which the strategy succeeds with probability strictly less than $1/2$.

### My solution

Switch if the observed value $x$ satisfies $x<T=t$, and do not switch otherwise.

Let $I$ be $1$ if the threshold strategy wins, and $0$ otherwise. Fix the two envelope values and call them $x$ and $y$, with $x\ne y$.

Case 1: $t$ is between $x$ and $y$.

$$
\mathbb{E}(I\mid x<t<y \text{ or } y<t<x)=1.
$$

In this case the threshold strategy is always correct. If we observe the smaller value, it is below $t$, so we switch to the larger value. If we observe the larger value, it is above $t$, so we do not switch.

Case 2: $t$ is greater than or equal to both $x$ and $y$.

$$
\mathbb{E}(I\mid x,y\le t)=\frac{1}{2}.
$$

In this case the strategy always switches. Since the originally chosen envelope is equally likely to be the larger or smaller one, switching wins with probability $1/2$.

Case 3: $t$ is less than or equal to both $x$ and $y$.

$$
\mathbb{E}(I\mid t\le x,y)=\frac{1}{2}.
$$

In this case the strategy never switches. Again, the originally chosen envelope is equally likely to be the larger or smaller one, so staying wins with probability $1/2$.

Therefore, the strategy wins for sure when $t$ falls between the two envelope values, and wins with probability $1/2$ in the other two cases. Since $T\sim \mathrm{Expo}(1)$ has positive density on every positive interval,

$$
\mathbb{P}(\min(x,y)<T<\max(x,y))>0.
$$

So, conditional on the two distinct envelope values $x$ and $y$,

$$
\mathbb{P}(I=1\mid x,y)
=\frac{1}{2}
+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}(\min(x,y)<T<\max(x,y))
>\frac{1}{2}.
$$

Thus the threshold strategy succeeds in picking the envelope with more money with probability strictly greater than $1/2$.

### What I initially missed / corrected

The strict improvement does not come from $T$ taking some exact value with positive probability. Since $T$ is continuous, every exact value has probability $0$.

The strict improvement comes from the interval between the two envelope values:

$$
\mathbb{P}(\min(x,y)<T<\max(x,y))>0.
$$

That is the event where the threshold separates the smaller envelope from the larger envelope.

### Book's solution (for comparison)

Source status: verified. The user provided the book excerpt for this problem.

Let $a$ be the smaller envelope value and $b$ be the larger envelope value. Assume $a<b$; if $a=b$, it makes no difference which envelope we choose.

Let $G$ be the event that the threshold strategy succeeds, and let $A$ be the event that we initially choose the envelope containing $a$.

If $A$ occurs, then we first see the smaller value $a$. The strategy succeeds exactly when we switch, which happens when $T>a$. Since $T\sim \mathrm{Expo}(1)$,

$$
\mathbb{P}(G\mid A)
=\mathbb{P}(T>a)
=e^{-a}.
$$

If $A^c$ occurs, then we first see the larger value $b$. The strategy succeeds exactly when we do not switch, which happens when $T\le b$. Therefore,

$$
\mathbb{P}(G\mid A^c)
=\mathbb{P}(T\le b)
=1-e^{-b}.
$$

The initial envelope is chosen at random, so

$$
\mathbb{P}(A)=\mathbb{P}(A^c)=\frac{1}{2}.
$$

By conditioning on whether we initially chose the smaller or larger envelope,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{P}(G)
&=\mathbb{P}(G\mid A)\mathbb{P}(A)
 +\mathbb{P}(G\mid A^c)\mathbb{P}(A^c)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}e^{-a}
 +\frac{1}{2}(1-e^{-b})\\
&=\frac{1}{2}
 +\frac{1}{2}(e^{-a}-e^{-b}).
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $a<b$, we have $e^{-a}>e^{-b}$. Thus

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb{P}(G)
=\frac{1}{2}
 +\frac{1}{2}(e^{-a}-e^{-b})
>\frac{1}{2}.
}
$$

The book's formula matches the threshold case split because

$$
e^{-a}-e^{-b}
=\mathbb{P}(a<T<b).
$$

### Intuition

The threshold is useful only if it lands between the two envelope values.

If $a<T<b$, then the threshold acts like a divider:

- seeing $a$ tells us to switch;
- seeing $b$ tells us to stay.

So in that case the strategy wins for sure. If $T\le a$ or $T\ge b$, the threshold does not distinguish the two values, so the strategy behaves like a blind always-stay or always-switch rule and wins with probability $1/2$.

### Memory card (quick review)

- The threshold strategy is: switch if the observed value is below $T$.
- The winning event improves over chance only when $T$ lands between the smaller and larger envelope values.
- For fixed $a<b$:

  $$
  \mathbb{P}(\text{win})
  =\frac{1}{2}
  +\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}(a<T<b).
  $$

- Since $T\sim \mathrm{Expo}(1)$,

  $$
  \mathbb{P}(a<T<b)=e^{-a}-e^{-b}>0.
  $$

## Strategic Practice 9 — Conditional Expectation Problem 4

**Prompt.**
You are given an amazing opportunity to bid on a mystery box containing a mystery prize. The value of the prize is completely unknown, except that it is worth at least nothing and at most a million dollars. So the true value $V$ of the prize is considered to be $\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$, measured in millions of dollars.

You can choose to bid any amount $b$, in millions of dollars. You have the chance to get the prize for considerably less than it is worth, but you could also lose money if you bid too much. Specifically, if $b < \frac{2}{3}V$, then the bid is rejected and nothing is gained or lost. If $b \ge \frac{2}{3}V$, then the bid is accepted and your net payoff is $V-b$, since you pay $b$ to get a prize worth $V$. What is your optimal bid $b$ to maximize the expected payoff?
