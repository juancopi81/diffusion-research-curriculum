Score-Matching Loss Floor

A score model that trains on noisy pairs has an expected training loss that cannot go below a fixed number. This page records how we derived that number for your Week 8 toy, what it means for your results, and what to check before you write.

\[ \underbrace{\tfrac1d\,\mathbb{E}\big\|y - s(x_t)\big\|^2}_{\text{training loss}} \;=\; \underbrace{\tfrac1d\,\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov}(y \mid x_t)\big]}_{\text{floor}\;\approx\;2.11} \;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac1d\,\mathbb{E}\big\|\nabla_{x_t}\log p_\sigma(x_t) - s(x_t)\big\|^2}_{\text{score error}} \]

Expectations are over fresh noisy pairs. The factor 1/d (d = 2) averages over coordinates, as nn.MSELoss does, so every number on this page is per coordinate.

Step 1

The engine: add and subtract

Why can no score model reach zero training loss?

The score model sees only \(x_t\). Many pairs \((x_0, \varepsilon)\) give the same \(x_t\), and each pair has its own target \(y\). Use the same move as in Denoising Is Not Inversion: add and subtract \(m(x_t) = \mathbb{E}[y \mid x_t]\) inside the square, then expand.

\[ \tfrac1d\,\mathbb{E}\|y-s\|^2 = \tfrac1d\,\mathbb{E}\|y-m\|^2 + \tfrac2d\,\mathbb{E}\big[(y-m)^{\top}(m-s)\big] + \tfrac1d\,\mathbb{E}\|m-s\|^2 \]
  • Cross term. Condition on \(x_t\) (Adam's law). Then \(m - s\) is fixed, so it moves out of the inner expectation. What remains is \(\mathbb{E}[y - m \mid x_t] = 0\).
  • First term. By Adam's law again, \(\tfrac1d\,\mathbb{E}\|y-m\|^2 = \tfrac1d\,\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov}(y \mid x_t)]\): the conditional variances of the coordinates, averaged. It depends only on the data and on σ. The model cannot change it. This is the floor.
  • Last term. The score error. Training can reduce it.

The squares are squared norms, which sum the d = 2 coordinates, and the cross term is an inner product. The factor 1/d turns the sums into per-coordinate averages, as nn.MSELoss does. You can also read the whole argument one coordinate at a time.

Key point

The floor exists because the model sees only \(x_t\), and pairs that share an \(x_t\) have different targets.

Two pairs, one input

\(x_0\) is 0 or 2. Noise \(e\) is −1 or +1. We observe \(x_t = 1\). Pair a: \(x_0 = 0, e = +1\). Pair b: \(x_0 = 2, e = -1\). Here the target is \(x_0\), so \(m = \mathbb{E}[x_0 \mid x_t = 1] = 1\).

Floor (Var = 1) Score error \((c - 1)^2\)

The score target gives the same picture. With \(y = -e\), pair a has target −1 and pair b has target +1. The floor is again 1.

Watch out

If the model also sees \(x_0\), the cross term is not zero. We still condition on \(x_t\) only, and \(x_0\) is still random given \(x_t\), so \(m - g(x_t, x_0)\) cannot move out. Try the checkbox: the cross term becomes −1 per pair and the loss becomes 0.

Step 2

What m(xₜ) is

If the label is y, what does the score model learn?

  1. From \(x_t = x_0 + \sigma\varepsilon\): \(y = -\varepsilon/\sigma = (x_0 - x_t)/\sigma^2\).
  2. This is the conditional score \(\nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t \mid x_0)\). It points from \(x_t\) back to the one \(x_0\) that made it.
  3. Identity (4) from Averaging Paths gives the average over all plausible \(x_0\):
\[ m(x_t) = \mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t \mid x_0) \,\big|\, x_t\big] = \nabla_{x_t}\log p_\sigma(x_t) \]

So \(m(x_t)\) is the marginal score: the analytical score in evaluate.py. The label \(y\) is not the analytical score. Steps 1 and 2 together give:

\[ \text{training loss} = \text{floor} + \underbrace{\tfrac1d\,\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{x_t}\log p_\sigma(x_t) - s(x_t)\|^2}_{\text{score MSE in evaluate.py}} \]

Many targets, one average

Each thin line goes from \(x_t\) to one plausible \(x_0\): a conditional score target, drawn at scale σ². The thick arrow is their average: the marginal score at the same scale. The spread of the thin lines around the thick arrow is the floor at this \(x_t\).

Step 3

The budget: Eve's law on the target

How big is the floor compared with the whole loss?

Steps 3 and 4 work with one coordinate at a time, so \(y\), \(\varepsilon\) and the scores are scalars. Eve's law holds for each coordinate. Averaging the two coordinates gives the per-coordinate numbers on this page.

Eve's law (Week 7 note, lines 283–310) with \(Y = y\) and \(X = x_t\):

\[ \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(y)}_{\text{predict-zero loss}} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Var}(y \mid x_t)]}_{\text{floor}} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(\mathbb{E}[y \mid x_t])}_{\text{earned back}} \]
  • \(\mathbb{E}[y] = 0\), so \(\operatorname{Var}(y) = \mathbb{E}[y^2] = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^2]/\sigma^2 = 1/\sigma^2 = 4\). This is the loss of a model that always predicts 0.
  • \(\mathbb{E}[y \mid x_t]\) is the marginal score. Its mean is 0 (Adam's law), so its variance is its expected squared size. This is what the exact score earns back: ≈ 1.89.
  • So 4 = floor (≈ 2.11) + earned back (≈ 1.89). The bar at the top of the page shows this split.

One more line connects the floor to the clean data. Given \(x_t\), the shift by \(x_t\) does not change a variance, and the factor \(1/\sigma^2\) comes out squared:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(y \mid x_t) = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(x_0 \mid x_t)}{\sigma^4} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{floor} = \frac{\tfrac1d\,\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov}(x_0 \mid x_t)]}{\sigma^4} \]

The left equation is for one coordinate. The right one averages it over the d = 2 coordinates.

Step 4

Inside the floor: the 2.0 by hand

Where does the number 2.11 come from?

Let \(k\) be the hidden label of the blob that produced \(x_0\). Take one coordinate of \(x_0\) and apply Eve's law again, with everything also conditioned on \(x_t\):

\[ \operatorname{Var}(x_0 \mid x_t) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{Var}(x_0 \mid x_t, k) \,\big|\, x_t\big]}_{\text{within-blob}} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}\big(\mathbb{E}[x_0 \mid x_t, k] \,\big|\, x_t\big)}_{\text{which-blob}} \]
  • Within-blob. Given \(k\), \(x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_k, \tau^2)\) and \(x_t = x_0 + \sigma\varepsilon\). Use the posterior variance from your Week 4 note with α = 1, \(s_0 = \tau = 0.5\), σ = 0.5: \[ \frac{\tau^2\sigma^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2} = \frac{0.0625}{0.5} = 0.125 \] It does not depend on \(x_t\) or on \(k\). Divide by \(\sigma^4 = 0.0625\): exactly 2.0.
  • Which-blob. Each blob gives its own guess \(\mathbb{E}[x_0 \mid x_t, k]\). This term is the spread of those guesses, weighted by the responsibilities \(r_k(x_t)\) from your Two Ways post. Divide it by \(\sigma^4\) too. Averaged over \(x_t\) and over the two coordinates, it adds ≈ 0.11 (Monte Carlo).
\[ \text{floor} = 2.0 + \frac{1}{\sigma^4}\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\operatorname{Var}\big(\mathbb{E}[x_0 \mid x_t, k] \,\big|\, x_t\big)\Big] \approx 2.0 + 0.11 = 2.11 \]

Three independent Monte Carlo runs gave 2.110, 2.115 and 2.1148. The which-blob part is small because the blobs are far apart. The closest centers, (0, 2) and (2, −1), are about 3.6 apart. The noisy blob width is \(\sqrt{\tau^2+\sigma^2} \approx 0.71\), so they are about 5 widths apart. The which-blob part is large only in thin bands between blobs, and few \(x_t\) land there.

Side note

The within-blob variance does not depend on \(x_t\). This is a general Gaussian fact: conditioning moves the mean, not the uncertainty. In the post, give it one sentence at most.

Step 5

What training can and cannot reduce

What does the identity say about longer training?

Your Week 13 note (lines 975–1018) names three causes of a gap from the ideal score: finite data, finite model capacity, and incomplete optimization.

  • All three act on the score error. They decide how far \(s\) ends up from \(m\). They do not change \(m\); the data and σ fix \(m\).
  • The floor is not one of them. It stays even with infinite data, a perfect model, and perfect optimization.
  • The lowest possible expected training loss, over fresh noisy pairs, is the whole floor, ≈ 2.11. The 2.0 is only the within-blob part.
  • A loss measured on one fixed, finite set is different. It can land below ≈ 2.11 by chance, and a flexible model trained on fixed noisy pairs could even memorize their noise. Your Week 8 loop draws fresh noise at every update (train.py line 126), so it cannot memorize the noise. That is why its training loss stays near the floor instead of sinking under it.

For the Two Ways open question about longer training: more training can reduce only the score error. It can never take the expected score-objective loss below ≈ 2.11. This says nothing about which objective wins.

Step 6

Your numbers, and what they can show

Can you subtract the floor from your Week 8 losses?

Part A · Units

The \(x_0\) model outputs \(g(x_t)\). evaluate.py converts it to an implied score \(\hat s = (g(x_t) - x_t)/\sigma^2\). With \(y = (x_0 - x_t)/\sigma^2\):

\[ \|\hat s - y\|^2 = \frac{\|g(x_t) - x_0\|^2}{\sigma^4} \]

So the \(x_0\) loss is σ⁴ times the score loss of its implied score. Divide it by σ⁴ = 0.0625 to put both losses in score units.

The one check (no new training)

Freeze the trained model. Draw one set of fresh held-out noisy pairs, and use the same clean points and the same noise for all three numbers: (1) the loss of the exact score against y, (2) your model's loss against y, and (3) your model's score MSE. Expect (2) − (1) ≈ (3).

Reuse gaussian_mixture_score from evaluate.py for (1). Number (1) is the floor measured on that set; it will be near 2.11 but not equal to it.

A larger set makes the agreement more reliable on average, because the cross term shrinks. One run with a larger set need not agree better than one with a smaller set. Fresh pairs matter: the model must not have trained on that noise.

Open item: how the floor changes with σ

What fraction of the predict-zero loss is floor, as σ → 0 and as σ → ∞? Start with the within-blob part. Check value: at σ = 0.5 the whole floor is 2.11 / 4 ≈ 53% of the predict-zero loss.

Show the answer (try it first)

Within-blob part only. Divide the within-blob floor by the predict-zero loss:

\[ \frac{\tau^2\sigma^2/(\tau^2+\sigma^2)\,/\,\sigma^4}{1/\sigma^2} = \frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2} \]

It goes to 1 as σ → 0 and to 0 as σ → ∞. At σ = 0.5 it is 0.5.

Whole floor. Per coordinate, the fraction is \(\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Var}(x_0 \mid x_t)]/\sigma^2\).

  • σ → 0: \(x_t\) pins \(x_0\) to within about σ, so \(\operatorname{Var}(x_0 \mid x_t) \approx \sigma^2\) and the fraction goes to 1. Almost all of the training loss is floor. A falling loss curve shows only a small remaining part.
  • σ → ∞: \(\operatorname{Var}(x_0 \mid x_t)\) goes to \(\operatorname{Var}(x_0)\), which stays finite, so the fraction goes to 0.
